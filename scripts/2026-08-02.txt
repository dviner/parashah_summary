Parashah: Re'eh (Deuteronomy 11:26-16:17)
Date: 2026-08-02

Welcome to Parashah in Brief, and thank you so much for joining me this week. Today we're looking at Parashat Re'eh, which means "See," a powerful portion from the book of Deuteronomy where Moses continues his farewell address to the Israelites as they stand on the edge of the Promised Land.

<break time="1.5s" />

Here is our summary of Deuteronomy, chapter eleven, verse twenty-six, through chapter sixteen, verse seventeen. Moses opens with a dramatic declaration: "See, I set before you today blessing and curse." Blessing will come if the people follow God's commandments, and curse if they turn away to worship other gods. He instructs them that once they cross the Jordan, they must destroy every pagan worship site they find — tear down the altars, smash the pillars, burn the sacred posts — and instead worship only at the one place God will choose as a central sanctuary. There they will bring their offerings and their tithes, and there they will feast and rejoice together with their families, their servants, and the Levites, who have no land of their own. Moses then warns against false prophets who perform signs and wonders but try to lead the people astray, and even against a beloved family member who secretly whispers, "Come, let us worship other gods." The portion lays out detailed dietary laws — which animals, fish, and birds may be eaten and which may not — reminding the people that they are a holy nation, chosen and treasured by God. Moses establishes a system of tithes, including a special tithe every third year set aside for the Levite, the stranger, the orphan, and the widow. He commands a release of debts every seventh year and urges open-handed generosity, saying, "Do not harden your heart against your needy kindred." Hebrew servants must be freed after six years and sent off with generous provisions, a reminder that the Israelites themselves were once slaves in Egypt. Finally, Moses outlines the three pilgrimage festivals — Passover with its unleavened bread recalling the hurried departure from Egypt, the Feast of Weeks celebrating the grain harvest, and the Feast of Booths after the autumn ingathering — when all shall appear before God and none shall come empty-handed.
