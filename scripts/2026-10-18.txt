Parashah: Lech-Lecha (Genesis 12:1-17:27)
Date: 2026-10-18

Welcome back to Parashah in Brief, so glad you're here. This week's portion is called Lech-Lecha, which means "Go forth," and it marks a turning point in the entire Torah — the moment we move from the universal story of humanity to the personal journey of one family that will change the world.

<break time="1.5s" />

Here is our summary of Genesis, chapter twelve, verse one, through chapter seventeen, verse twenty-seven. The portion opens with God calling out to a man named Abram, telling him to leave his homeland and travel to an unknown land, with the extraordinary promise that he will become a great nation and a blessing to all the families of the earth. Abram obeys — at seventy-five years old — and sets out with his wife Sarai and his nephew Lot toward the land of Canaan. When they arrive, God appears to Abram and promises the land to his offspring, and Abram builds altars along the way. But famine strikes, forcing Abram down to Egypt, where he asks Sarai to pose as his sister out of fear for his life — a plan that lands Sarai in Pharaoh's palace until God sends plagues and the truth comes out. Back in Canaan, Abram and Lot part ways because the land cannot support both their growing households, and Lot chooses the lush Jordan plain near the wicked city of Sodom. Soon after, a coalition of kings sweeps through the region, captures Sodom, and takes Lot prisoner — so Abram musters three hundred and eighteen men, launches a daring nighttime rescue, and brings everyone home. In a deeply mystical scene, God then seals a formal covenant with Abram, promising that his descendants will be as countless as the stars, though they will first endure slavery in a foreign land for four hundred years before being freed. Meanwhile, Sarai, still childless, gives her Egyptian maidservant Hagar to Abram, and Hagar bears a son named Ishmael, a name meaning "God heeds." Finally, when Abram is ninety-nine, God appears again, changes his name to Abraham — meaning father of a multitude — and Sarai's name to Sarah, meaning princess, and establishes circumcision as the sign of an everlasting covenant, promising that Sarah herself will bear a son named Isaac.
