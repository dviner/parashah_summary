Parashah: Ki Tavo (Deuteronomy 26:1-29:8)
Date: 2026-08-23

Welcome back to Parashah in Brief, and thanks so much for joining me this week. We're diving into Parashat Ki Tavo, which means "when you come in," and it's found near the end of Deuteronomy as the Israelites stand on the very edge of the Promised Land, receiving Moses's final instructions before they cross the Jordan.

Here is our summary of Deuteronomy, chapter twenty-six, verse one, through chapter twenty-nine, verse eight. Moses tells the people that once they enter and settle the land, they must gather their first fruits — the very first produce of the soil — place them in a basket, and bring them to the priest at God's chosen place. When they present these fruits, they are to recite a powerful, compressed retelling of their story: "My father was a fugitive Aramean, he went down to Egypt, we became a great nation, the Egyptians oppressed us, and God freed us with a mighty hand and brought us to this land flowing with milk and honey." It's a moment of gratitude, a way of saying, "I haven't forgotten where we came from." Moses then instructs that every third year, a special tithe must be set aside for the Levites, the strangers, the orphans, and the widows, ensuring no one in the community goes hungry. Next, Moses lays out a dramatic ceremony for when the people cross the Jordan: they are to set up large stones on Mount Ebal, coat them with plaster, and inscribe all the words of the Teaching upon them. Six tribes will stand on Mount Gerizim to pronounce blessings, and six on Mount Ebal for the curses. The Levites then call out a series of twelve curses — against idol worship, dishonoring parents, moving a neighbor's boundary marker, misleading the blind, perverting justice, and other secret sins — and after each one, all the people respond, "Amen." Then comes one of the most striking passages in all of Torah: an extended recitation of blessings and curses. If the people faithfully follow God's commandments, they will be blessed in the city and the country, in their families and their fields, and God will make them the head and not the tail. But if they turn away, the curses are devastating — disease, drought, defeat, exile, and suffering described in agonizing detail, culminating in being scattered among the nations with no rest and no peace. The portion closes with Moses reminding the people of everything God did for them — from the plagues in Egypt, through forty years in the wilderness where their clothes never wore out, to their victories over the kings Sihon and Og — urging them to observe the covenant faithfully so that they may succeed in all they undertake.
