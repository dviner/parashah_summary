Parashah: Toldot (Genesis 25:19-28:9)
Date: 2026-11-08

Welcome to Parashah in Brief! I'm so glad you're here with us this week. Today we're looking at Parashat Toldot, which means "generations" or "offspring," and it picks up the story of Abraham's family by turning the spotlight to his son Isaac and the dramatic rivalry between Isaac's twin sons.

<break time="1.5s" />

Here is our summary of Genesis, chapter twenty-five, verse nineteen, through chapter twenty-eight, verse nine. The portion opens with Isaac and his wife Rebekah struggling with infertility. Isaac prays to God, and Rebekah conceives — but the pregnancy is turbulent, and God tells her that two nations are wrestling inside her womb, and that the older child will serve the younger. Twins are born: first Esau, red and hairy, and then Jacob, gripping his brother's heel. As the boys grow up, Esau becomes a rugged hunter favored by Isaac, while Jacob is a quiet, thoughtful man favored by Rebekah. One day Esau comes home famished and sells his birthright — his special rights as the firstborn — to Jacob for nothing more than a bowl of lentil stew. The story then follows Isaac through a famine: God tells him to stay in the land rather than go to Egypt, reaffirming the covenant promises made to Abraham. Isaac grows wealthy in the region of Gerar, digs wells, clashes with the local Philistines, and eventually makes a peace treaty with their king Abimelech. Then comes the portion's most dramatic scene. Isaac, now old and nearly blind, prepares to give his deathbed blessing to Esau, but Rebekah orchestrates a bold deception: she dresses Jacob in Esau's clothes and covers his smooth skin with goatskins so he will feel hairy to his father's touch. Jacob goes in, impersonates his brother, and receives the powerful blessing of abundance and dominion. When Esau returns and discovers what has happened, he lets out a bitter, heartbreaking cry and vows to kill Jacob. Rebekah, hearing of Esau's rage, urges Jacob to flee to her brother Laban in Haran, and Isaac sends him off with a final blessing — the blessing of Abraham — charging him to find a wife from among their own family rather than from the Canaanite women.
