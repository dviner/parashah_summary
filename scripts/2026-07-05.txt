Parashah: Matot-Masei (Numbers 30:2-36:13)
Date: 2026-07-05

Welcome back to Parashah in Brief! This week we have a double portion called Matot-Masei, meaning "Tribes" and "Journeys." This is the final reading in the book of Numbers, and it brings the Israelites right to the edge of the Promised Land, closing out their long wilderness wandering.

<break time="1.5s" />

Here is our summary of Numbers, chapter thirty, verse two, through chapter thirty-six, verse thirteen. The portion opens with laws about vows, emphasizing that when a person makes a pledge to God, their word is binding and must not be broken. We learn that a father or husband may annul the vows of a daughter or wife under certain conditions, but a widow or divorced woman's vow stands entirely on her own authority. God then commands Moses to wage war against the Midianites as retribution for their role in leading Israel astray at Peor. Twelve thousand soldiers go out, a thousand from each tribe, led by Phinehas the priest, and they defeat the five kings of Midian and also slay the prophet Balaam. After the battle, the spoils are carefully divided between the warriors and the rest of the community, with portions set aside for the priests and the Levites. Then the tribes of Reuben and Gad approach Moses with a surprising request: they want to settle on the east side of the Jordan River, where the land is ideal for their vast herds of cattle. Moses is furious at first, fearing a repeat of the catastrophic spy episode that condemned the previous generation to wander forty years. But the two tribes promise to serve as front-line soldiers and help conquer the land west of the Jordan before returning to their homes, and Moses agrees. The second half of the portion, Masei, opens with a detailed travel log listing all forty-two encampments from Egypt to the plains of Moab, a sweeping recap of the entire journey. God then gives instructions for conquering and dividing the land of Canaan, establishes its boundaries, designates six cities of refuge for those who accidentally take a life, and sets rules to protect tribal land inheritance.
